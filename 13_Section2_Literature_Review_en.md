## 2.1 The dialogic tradition: withholding the answer is the oldest idea in the field

The pedagogical rationale for not giving the answer predates computing by two and a half millennia. In Plato's *Theaetetus*, Socrates describes his practice as *maieutics* — intellectual midwifery — and explicitly disclaims being a source of content: the teacher does not implant knowledge but assists the learner in bringing forth what the learner can already reach. The Confucian parallel is equally explicit about the *timing* of assistance. *Analects* 7.8 records the condition under which instruction should occur — instruction is withheld until the learner has strained toward the problem and failed to articulate it (不愤不启，不悱不发) — making the withholding decision conditional on an estimate of the learner's state rather than on the teacher's convenience. We quote the *Analects* itself here rather than a secondary commentary on it. We also state the convergence narrowly, because the comparative literature on these two traditions ordinarily emphasises what separates them: Tweed and Lehman (2002) characterise the contrast as Socratic questioning of received knowledge on the one hand and Confucian effortful, respectful acquisition of it on the other. What the two texts share is the *timing condition* on assistance, not the aims of learning. Freire (1970) supplied the modern critical version, naming the transmission of finished content "banking education" and arguing that depositing answers into a passive learner substitutes for, and thereby prevents, the learner's own reconstruction of meaning.

Three traditions separated by geography, era, and epistemology converge on the same structural claim: **the answer is withheld not because the teacher lacks it but because delivering it short-circuits the process that produces learning.** That claim is the entire normative basis for every guidance-oriented tutor that follows, including this one.

Why the effort is worth making at all is an empirical question with a well-known answer. Bloom (1984) reported that one-to-one tutoring moves the average learner roughly two standard deviations above the performance of conventional group instruction — the "2 sigma problem" — and framed the search for scalable methods that approach that effect as the central problem of instructional research. The result is cited here as motivation and nothing more: it is an effect of human tutoring under mastery learning, it is not a target this paper approaches, and nothing in §6 should be read against it. Its relevance is narrower and still substantial: it establishes that how a tutor behaves during an interaction is a first-order determinant of what learners gain, which is what makes the composition of that behaviour worth decomposing.

What the tradition does not supply is a mechanism for its central condition. Both Socrates and Confucius assume a teacher who can *tell* when the learner has strained and failed — an assumption that is trivially satisfied when the teacher is a person in a room and is not satisfied at all when the tutor is a language model serving thousands of students. Automating the dialogic tradition therefore converts a judgement into a design variable, and the field's subsequent history is largely the history of where that variable was placed.

## 2.2 Generative AI in the classroom: the answer-oriented default and the performance/learning disconnect

Contemporary LLM tutors default to answering. The default is not a neutral implementation choice, and the evidence that it is harmful is now available at more than one scale.

At the classroom scale, Brender et al. (2026) report in their opening section that gains observed among students using LLM assistants are "neither indicative of students' ability to perform without the tool nor of improved learning outcomes" (§1.1). That observation has since been established experimentally at scale. Liu, Christian, Dumbalska, Bakker and Dubey (2026, arXiv:2604.04721) report a series of randomised controlled trials (N = 1,222) in which AI assistance improved performance while it was available, but participants subsequently performed significantly worse without assistance and were more likely to abandon the task; the divergence appears after roughly ten minutes of exposure. Because assignment is randomised and the outcome is measured after assistance is withdrawn, this is the causal form of the classroom-scale claim. A neurophysiological study reports a compatible pattern: Kosmyna et al. (2025, arXiv:2506.08872, MIT Media Lab) recorded essay writing under three conditions — LLM, search engine, and no tools — and found that brain connectivity scaled down systematically with the amount of external support, that self-reported ownership of the resulting essay was lowest in the LLM condition, and that LLM users had difficulty quoting from text they had produced minutes earlier. Its authors group these findings under the term *cognitive debt*. Two qualifications attach to that study and we record them because it is load-bearing for §1.1: it remains an unreviewed preprint whose own authors caution that its conclusions are preliminary, and the fourth-session crossover on which much of the interpretation rests involved 18 of the 54 participants.

The exposure to answers is not only passive. Learners work actively to obtain them, and the behaviour has been named and characterised in the cognitive-tutor literature as *gaming the system*: quickly and repeatedly requesting help until the tutor supplies the correct answer, often before the learner has attempted the problem at all (Baker, Corbett, Koedinger, & Wagner, 2004). Learners who game the system learn substantially less from the interaction than their on-task behaviour suggests. The point carries directly into design: a constraint that a learner can talk their way around is not a constraint, irrespective of which layer implements it (§2.3).

At cohort scale, the pattern is starker. In a first-year programming course of 600 students, Suvernev et al. (2026) report that as generative AI use became widespread, the mean exam score fell from 72% to 54% while the average homework grade — work done outside class, with the tool available — rose. Performance measured *with* the assistant improved; performance measured *without* it collapsed. The same authors conclude that prohibition is neither realistic nor defensible, which fixes the design obligation precisely: keep the tool, remove the shortcut.

**The strongest evidence for that obligation, and the one that most constrains this paper, is a randomised controlled trial.** Bastani et al. (2025, *PNAS*) randomised **classrooms** — about fifty 9th–11th-grade mathematics classes, nearly a thousand students in total, over four 90-minute sessions — across an unguarded chatbot (GPT Base), a guardrailed version of the same model whose prompt instructed it to "provide hints to the student without directly giving them the answer" and to guide step-by-step (GPT Tutor), and a business-as-usual control given books and notes but no device. Honours-designated classrooms, which are not populated randomly, were excluded from the main sample. Relative to control, performance on the assisted practice sessions rose by 48% under the unguarded tutor and by 127% under the guardrailed one; on the later exam taken without the tool, the unguarded condition fell **17% below control**, while the guardrailed condition was not statistically distinguishable from it. Two features of this study set it apart from everything else cited in this section. It is randomised rather than observational, so the practice-gain/later-loss split is not confounded with who chose to use the tool — assignment was at the classroom level and the reported standard errors are clustered accordingly. And it does not merely show that a tutor can be harmful — it shows that a guardrail can prevent the harm, on an outcome measured after the tool is withdrawn, in a design that compares guarded against unguarded directly.

It is therefore important to be exact about what this study does not do, because it is close enough to our question to be mistaken for an answer to it. Bastani et al. compare **having a guardrail with not having one**: the guardrail is applied as a whole intervention, and the contrast is treatment against control. That design establishes that the package helps. It cannot say what inside the package did the work — how much of the recovered later-test performance came from the guidance the guarded tutor supplied, and how much came merely from its no longer handing over the answer, since both changed at once. Their own framing makes the point: the intervention is described as a guardrail, and the outcome is a test score. A reader who concludes from it that *guidance* was the active ingredient has made the inference this paper exists to block. The same structure that makes their result the strongest evidence for the obligation also makes it the cleanest available instance of the package problem of §2.4.3 — and, because the trial is randomised and large, it is an instance that cannot be dismissed as a weak study.

Chinese-language scholarship reaches the same conclusion through a different route, and for a paper in this journal the domestic line of critique is not decoration. Sun Lihui (2026) analyses the mechanism rather than the outcome, arguing that the predictive logic of generative output produces an "excessive affirmation" (过量肯定) in the act of answering: when a student presses a mistaken line of questioning, the model does not contradict it, and the error consolidates. Li Jingjing and Wang Tianjian (2026) systematise the four stances educational practice has taken towards generative AI — prohibition, circumvention, integration, and creation — and argue that each carries a failure mode of its own, including the dependency the first two invite and the effacement of learner agency the last two risk. Wang Yiyang (2024) positions the agent as an extension of the teacher's judgement rather than a source of answers, within a teacher–machine–student ternary structure. Policy has already settled the direction of travel: the shift from knowledge transmission to capability development (能力提升为本) is stated national orientation, and we follow it rather than argue for it. Independently, in this journal, Sun et al. (2026) coded human–AI learning interaction patterns and identified an "answer-oriented mode" as the low-order pattern, recommending dialogue organised around questioning instead.

Two things follow, and the second is the one this paper is built on. First, there is no longer any serious dispute that answer-oriented assistance is a problem — and after Bastani et al. (2025) there is no serious dispute that guarding the tutor helps either. Second — and this is where the critique stops — **"the guardrail helped" is not the same claim as "the guidance inside it did the work."** What no study above establishes is how much of a guardrail's benefit comes from the guidance it supplies and how much comes merely from its no longer handing over the answer. Establishing that requires a design in which the two channels can be separated, and §2.4.3 argues that no existing system supplies one.

This is a narrow gap, and we would rather say so than let it sound larger than it is. The field has not failed to show that guardrails work; a classroom-randomised trial of nearly a thousand students has shown it, on an outcome measured after the tool was withdrawn. What it has not done is look inside that result.

## 2.3 Where the constraint sits: five placements, and why the label "Socratic" obscures the difference

Work on withholding answers is routinely gathered under the heading "Socratic tutoring." That heading is misleading. What distinguishes the systems is not whether they question, nor whether they invoke Socrates, but **which component of the pipeline enforces the withholding**. Five placements are represented in the literature — four of them architectural, the fifth a measurement and auditing layer that has grown around the other four — and the placement determines what the system can and cannot guarantee.

**(a) Prompt level.** The constraint is an instruction given to the model at generation time. SocraticLM (Liu et al., 2024, NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight) builds a Dean–Teacher–Student pipeline and a 35K-dialogue SocraTeach corpus, with a Dean agent reviewing the Teacher's utterances for Socratic style — including whether they avoid supplying the answer directly. Chudziak and Kostka (2025, AIED) implement a multi-agent mathematics tutor with dual memory, GraphRAG retrieval and DAG course planning, whose design principle is stated as guided learning "contrary to giving direct answers." Lee et al. (2026, AIED) deploy adaptive Socratic dialogue in an undergraduate CS course. Brender et al. (2026, AIED) run a six-week, 66-student quasi-experiment comparing a Socratic-guidance tutor with a prompt-refinement tutor. Consumer systems, including ChatGPT Study Mode, occupy the same layer. In every case the enforcement mechanism is the model's instruction-following.

**(b) Output post-processing and input validation.** The constraint is a check applied around the model rather than inside it. Thonge and Thakkar (2026, ITiCSE) present Socratic AI, a VS Code–integrated tutor whose dialogue is, in their words, "constrained to withhold direct solutions," on the stated ground that generating correct solutions instantly "undermines the struggle necessary for conceptual learning." Those two clauses are the whole of what the published abstract (46 words; §2.7) supports. It does not say *where* the constraint is enforced, so we place the paper in this category provisionally and flag the placement as unverified: VS Code integration is not by itself evidence that enforcement is tooling-side rather than prompt-side. We credit it with the design intent, not with a mechanism. Sunil and Thakkar (2025) validate the *student's* input for completeness and relevance before any feedback is issued — a constraint on the input side, which as they note leaves the tutor's own output unconstrained. Rule-based checking is stronger than instruction, but it is categorical: an output passes or is blocked. **The strongest instance of this placement that we have located is Pisan (2026, arXiv:2608.12292)**, and we name it separately rather than listing it, because it is the nearest prior work to our own question. Pisan adds a supervisor architecture over the model: a per-turn contract specifying what the tutor may and may not supply, enforcement by a deterministic checker together with an LLM judge, an eight-level help ladder, and an evidence-driven procedure for tuning how Socratic the tutor's behaviour is — in a deployed system. It makes withholding enforceable and tunable in a way that prompt-level designs are not, and it is deployed where our own work is simulated. We discuss the relation at length in §7.3.1 and concede there what we concede here: on the question of whether a tutor can be made to hold the answer, and whether the holding can be measured, this work has more to show than ours does. What it does not yield is the decomposition of §2.4.3, and that is the whole of what we claim.

**(c) Learner modelling.** The constraint is indirect — the system adapts to a model of the learner and thereby produces less answer-like output. IntelliCode (2025) couples six agents to a centralised learner model under graduated hinting. Chudziak and Kostka (2025) retrieve against a knowledge graph and plan over a DAG. In this journal, Wang et al. (2026) report a six-agent system with dynamic scheduling over a three-layer task–knowledge–thinking knowledge graph, evaluated across four quasi-experimental rounds for creative thinking. Learner modelling changes *what* is taught; it does not by itself bound *how much of the answer* is revealed.

**(d) Training objective.** The constraint enters the learning signal. Suvernev et al. (2026) train open-source hint models (1B–7B) for first-year programming using supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with GRPO, and an adversarial curriculum in which a stronger model generates increasingly difficult tasks and incorrect solutions to expose the tutor's weaknesses — a genuine advance over prompt-only designs and a direct demonstration that withholding can be trained. Their deployment pairs the hint models with OPTMentor, a debugger that incorporates a jailbreak-prevention system. SocraticPO (Liu et al., 2026) works on the adjacent *reward* side of a reinforcement-learning problem: it decays the reward for assisted success so that the **student policy** being trained does not learn to wait for help, with the decay self-determined from historical statistics rather than set by a tunable parameter. Its student is a policy rather than a learner and its evaluation is on benchmark tasks; that scope difference has to be carried whenever the paper is cited inside an educational argument, and we do not treat the absence of a tutoring instrument there as a gap in that work.

**(e) Leakage measurement and auditing as a subfield.** By 2026 answer leakage is no longer an unmeasured notion: it has benchmarks, deterministic detectors and pre-deployment audit protocols, and this paper sits *inside* that subfield rather than beside it. Zhao, Knežević and Käser (2026, ACL 2026) evaluate the leakage robustness of LLM tutors against adversarial student attacks, adapting six families of adversarial and persuasive techniques to the educational setting; finding in-context adversarial agents largely ineffective, they fine-tune a student agent to jailbreak tutors and propose it as the core of a standardised benchmark. Fan et al. (2026) run a pre-registered audit holding the base model and one weak simulated student fixed while contrasting a conversational against a pedagogical policy, scoring 1,179 answer-phase turns with a condition-blind primary judge and deterministic detectors for leakage and next-turn independent work; they report that answer-revealing turns are followed by less independent student work on every base, and that general-purpose helpfulness is not a reliable pedagogy signal. Kadir (2026) formalises pedagogical leakage as a state- and action-dependent failure, introduces an authorisation-aware release boundary over five disclosure contracts, and performs **matched component attribution** across selection, generation, verification and enforcement. ExplainRoute (Gai, Zhang and Huang, 2026) audits information boundaries for non-answer-giving tutors before deployment, exposing state, strategy and leakage risk through a machine-checkable contract, and states plainly that its contribution is a validated audit protocol and a boundary finding rather than evidence of causal instructional effectiveness. Pisan (2026) reports a deployed tutor that enforces withholding as a per-turn machine-checkable contract with a per-turn ceiling on an eight-rung help ladder, tuned against evidence using scripted student personas and no human subjects. EduGuard (Hossain et al., 2026, ICCA 2026) reports direct-answer leakage of 9.8% and a fall in overreliance from 38.0% to 17.0% in a ten-student counterbalanced pilot. In aggregate this subfield has produced measurement, benchmarking, adversarial robustness results and component-level failure attribution; §2.4.3 argues that the one thing it has not produced is a decomposition of an outcome gain into instructional channels.

> **Honest placement of the two training-layer systems (mandatory).** Suvernev et al. (2026) is a conference paper at EDULEARN26 and we have obtained its published abstract but not its full text. **According to that published abstract**, the safety mechanism is a jailbreak-prevention filter and the optimisation target is hint quality; no leakage measure appears in the abstract. We therefore do not write that this work involves no leakage measurement at all — only that we have not been able to verify one, and that the mechanism described in the abstract is a binary gate rather than a quantity. The full text remains behind a paywall and is listed in §2.7. SocraticPO is not a competitor: it constrains the *reward* side while leaving what the tutor is permitted to say under prompt instruction. We treat it as a reward-side counterpart to our content-side constraint, not as prior art we displace.

**The axis that matters.** Sorting the field by placement reveals that the interesting divide is not prompt versus training. It is **whether the design yields any bound at all on the answer information that a given configuration permits.** A prompt instruction yields none; a validator yields a binary verdict; a jailbreak filter, as described, yields a binary verdict; an adversarial curriculum improves robustness without reporting a quantity. Sharpening the check does not change this — even the strongest member of placement (b), Pisan's machine-checked per-turn contract, yields a *bound* on what is revealed, and not a decomposition of an observed improvement into a term for guidance and a term for withholding. The reason is structural rather than a matter of degree: a contract enforced identically on every turn leaves no condition in which withholding moves while guidance is held fixed, and without such a condition the two contributions cannot be separated however precisely the withholding is measured. The consequence is testable and has been tested: constraints that a learner can talk around are soft constraints regardless of how they are implemented. Brender et al. (2026, §1.2) observed that students "resisted restrictive interfaces" and "may actively bypass constraints to obtain full responses." Sunil and Thakkar (2025) independently recorded students attempting to "circumvent reflective prompts or reformulate prohibited solution requests" — a different course, a different mechanism, the same result. SocraticPO (2026, §5.4) states the mechanism directly: "even when the prompt explicitly asks the teacher not to reveal the answer, a teacher with ground-truth access may still leak solution-specific hints," and this failure is "inherently limited by the teacher model's instruction-following ability." The most recent instance is also the most systematic. Zhao, Knežević and Käser (2026), evaluating leakage robustness against adversarial student attacks at ACL 2026, find that a fine-tuned student agent succeeds in extracting answers where off-the-shelf in-context adversarial agents largely do not, and propose that agent as the core of a standardised benchmark (§2.3(e)). Circumvention is therefore not an anecdote about one deployment: it is a reproducible property of the tutor–learner pair, obtained by adversarially optimising the learner rather than by asking one to try.

Multi-turn interaction makes the matter worse rather than better, which matters directly here because guided dialogue is inherently multi-turn. Hazra et al. (2026), in SafeTutors, benchmark eleven models (ten open-weight plus one closed, 3.8B–72B) over 3,135 single-turn and 2,820 multi-turn dialogues constructed by escalating prompts; no model was broadly safe. On the pedagogical dimension, the cross-model mean harm rate rose from 17.7% in single-turn exchanges to 77.8% at five to eight turns, with answer over-disclosure, misconception reinforcement and abdication of scaffolding among the harm types; human validation by two raters on 900 single-turn and 300 multi-turn items gave κ = 0.76. Two qualifications must be carried with those numbers: they are the cross-model mean on one dimension rather than an overall failure rate, and they show that prompt-layer pedagogical compliance degrades with dialogue length rather than measuring leakage in information terms.

### 2.3.1 A parallel literature outside the educational-technology community

The axis of §2.3 is drawn from work published in educational-technology and computing-education venues. There is a second, largely disjoint literature on the same problem in the NLP community — ACL, BEA, EMNLP, NeurIPS — and a review that omits it can be read, fairly, as having surveyed half the field. Five items are close enough to require naming.

**STAP** (Xie, Shi, Li and Liu, 2025, ISAIE; a four-page system paper on novice programming) reframes the model's role "from an 'oracle' to a 'Socratic tutor'" via a four-stage pipeline — checking, correcting, complementing, segmenting — and devotes a section (§3.2, *Terminology and Formalization*) to **formalising three concepts it names as *Socratic hints*, minimum-viable-hint (MVH) levels, and *answer leakage*, supplying operational definitions and compliance criteria for each**. That formalisation predates the 2026 audit literature of §2.3(e), so the terminology this paper uses is not ours to introduce and we do not claim it — and the prior art is stronger than naming alone, since the criteria are operational. It is categorical nonetheless: a turn complies or it does not, which yields a score for a configuration rather than the response curve of §2.4.2. STAP's evaluation is scope-limited and formative by its own account: it reports adherence to pedagogical principles and motivates classroom studies rather than reporting outcomes.

**ScaffoldLM** (Li, Zhu, Wang, Li and Huang, 2026, ACL) generates a plan from solution steps to serve as a stable scaffolding backbone, then runs a control loop that infers the learner's cognitive state, evaluates whether the current step's target has been met, and adaptively selects the next action, in multi-turn mathematics dialogue tutoring. It is the closest system in this literature to coupling *stepwise scaffolding* to an *inferred learner state* — the same coupling that appears inside contribution (3) — and it appears as a published ACL long paper rather than a preprint. It is the reason §2.5 does not claim that coupling as a contribution.

**Petukhova, Nguyen and Kochmar (2026, BEA)** align a tutor for math mistake remediation by two-stage training — supervised fine-tuning followed by direct preference optimisation over synthetic preference pairs built along **scaffolding** and factuality dimensions — and state their pedagogical target as "guiding students **without revealing final answers**." Their human evaluation finds the best aligned open model competitive with a proprietary baseline, and they record the same difficulty this paper does: "challenges in reliably evaluating tutoring quality." This is the training-layer placement of §2.3(d) applied to withholding specifically, with preference data rather than a graded constraint.

**EduDial** (Wei, Zhang, Lin, Jiang, Dai and Kuang, 2026, Findings of EMNLP) contributes 34,250 multi-turn teacher–student dialogue sessions spanning 345 knowledge points of the K–12 mathematics curriculum, generated by teacher and student agents under Bloom's taxonomy and ten questioning strategies — including zone-of-proximal-development and metacognitive questioning — with an eleven-dimensional evaluation framework for teaching ability. **ToST** (Ling and Yu, 2026, preprint) establishes MPSG-Bench: 31K multi-path teaching dialogues scored on five dimensions grounded in SOLO theory, for guidance that admits multiple valid solution paths.

**What this literature changes for us.** Three things, none of them comfortable. First, *answer leakage* is a term we neither coined nor adopted from the 2026 audits: STAP formalised it inside a tutoring system in 2025, so the gap in §2.4.2 concerns **relationships**, not naming. Second, the training layer now includes preference-based alignment of withholding (Petukhova et al., 2026) alongside reinforcement learning (Suvernev et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), which leaves the *binary-versus-graded* distinction as the only axis on which our constraint differs from prior training-layer work. Third, instruments for teaching quality are being built at scale — EduDial's eleven dimensions, ToST's SOLO-grounded five, MPSG-Bench's 31K dialogues — which raises the bar on our own measurement choices rather than lowering it. What none of them supplies is still the object of §2.4.3: a decomposition of an outcome gain into instructional channels. ScaffoldLM comes closest on the design side, but a plan-conditioned controller is not a pair of independently switchable channels, and it reports no attribution of a gain.

## 2.4 Three gaps that the placement axis exposes

### 2.4.1 Guidance intensity is not exposed as a tunable parameter

Brender et al. (2026, §5.3) state plainly that observed effects "may depend on how the tutors were calibrated; different levels of guidance may lead to different interaction patterns and learning outcomes," and their §5.1 closes on the possibility that inducing learning-conducive engagement "may require stronger or differently calibrated forms of guidance." This establishes that intensity is a design variable with non-trivial structure. It does not establish what that structure is: the paper compares two calibrations and reports neither a curve nor an optimum, and no reading of it as support for a particular functional form is warranted. The gap here is narrower than "nobody exposes intensity." Pisan (2026) exposes it: a per-turn ceiling on an eight-rung help ladder, tuned against evidence until four acceptance criteria were met. What is not exposed is the *continuous* form — the ladder is stepped, and it is tuned against acceptance criteria rather than against a leakage rate. Thonge and Thakkar (2026), whose published abstract is all we hold, state the withholding constraint qualitatively rather than as an adjustable quantity; SocraticPO's decay is data-driven and has no control knob; Suvernev et al.'s (2026) gate, as described in the abstract, is binary. No system in §2.3 treats the amount of permitted answer information as a continuously adjustable quantity.

### 2.4.2 Leakage is scored, but not measured against a tunable constraint

It would be wrong to write that answer disclosure has gone unmeasured. *Telling@N*, from MathDial (Macina et al., 2023), is a standard measure of the proportion of turns in which a tutor reveals the answer, and Chudziak and Kostka (2025) use it to compare a tutor prompt against a base prompt. SafeTutors (Hazra et al., 2026) includes a single-turn pedagogical item, "gives away answers or not," coded binary, alongside a 0–5 ordinal `challenges_learner` scale.

What is absent is different and specific, and it has to be stated against a subfield that now measures leakage well. Existing uses of these instruments produce **a score for a configuration**: Chudziak and Kostka compare two prompting strategies, and SafeTutors scores models. The 2026 work goes considerably further — Fan et al. (2026) measure leakage and next-turn independent work with deterministic detectors under a pre-registered design; Zhao et al. (2026) measure leakage under adversarial attack across model families, pedagogically aligned models and a multi-agent design; Wang et al. (2026) report keyword leakage falling from 30.0% to 13.3% across GRPO alignment; Hossain et al. (2026) report direct-answer leakage of 9.8% — but every one of these attaches a rate to a fixed system, or contrasts two fixed policies. None varies a constraint and plots the response. Sunil and Thakkar (2025) sit at the other extreme, monitoring query volume, reflection quality and escalation frequency while applying no measure at all to tutor output. The defensible formulation is therefore narrow: **answer disclosure is now routinely measured and benchmarked; what no existing system provides is a relationship between a continuously adjustable constraint strength and an observed leakage rate.** We withdraw an inference we had previously drawn: we had treated the absence of the term *Telling@N* in a reinforcement-learning paper as evidence of a gap in the field. It is not — that instrument belongs to dialogue tutoring and not to policy optimisation, and its absence there carries no information about the state of tutoring research. In this paper that relationship is characterised in a **simulation study under a stipulated learner model**. We make **no classroom claim**: the simulation is presented as a property of the apparatus — that the decomposition is well-defined and that the diagnostic rejects models in which the attribution does not hold — and every quantity it produces is labelled as simulated throughout §§5–6.

### 2.4.3 A successful package does not establish attribution — and this is the gap the field named

The third gap is methodological and, in our reading, decisive. Weidlich, Gašević, Drachsler and Kirschner (2025, *JCAL* 41(5), e70105) audited the nineteen comparisons inside the Deng et al. (2025) meta-analysis against three non-negotiable conditions — reproducible treatment, operationalised control, valid measurement of learning. Reproducibility was met by 74%, operationalised controls by 42%, and valid learning measurement by 53%; **all three simultaneously by four comparisons, 21%.** Their conclusion is that "observed gains cannot, at this time, be confidently attributed to ChatGPT," and their prescription is to "measure learning after the treatment is removed."

That prescription bites on the systems reviewed above because several of them move leakage and learning at the same time. Any tutor that withholds more answers and simultaneously produces better post-test scores has established a correlation between two things it varied together. The randomised evidence reviewed in §2.2 shows how sharp the problem is. Bastani et al. (2025) found that giving students an unguarded chatbot raised their practice scores and *lowered* their scores on a later test taken without the tool, so the apparent gain reversed once assistance was removed; this is not an argument about whether gains transfer but a demonstration that gains measured under assistance do not. Their guarded condition recovers the later-test outcome, and this is exactly where the package problem bites hardest — and it bites harder than a two-channel reading suggests. By the authors' own description of the prompt, the guarded condition differs from the unguarded one in **three** respects simultaneously: it instructs the model to supply hints rather than the answer, it requires step-by-step guidance, and it injects teacher-provided correct solutions, common student mistakes and prescribed feedback — the last of which removes a source of error the unguarded tutor retains, since the authors report that GPT Base "often provides incorrect answers due to hallucinations." The recovered later-test outcome therefore cannot be divided between guidance, withholding and feedback accuracy by that design, however large and well-conducted the trial is. One further fact is worth carrying rather than suppressing: the guardrail is implemented **entirely in the prompt**, which places the strongest available evidence that guardrails work in the prompt-level placement of §2.3(a) — the placement whose guarantee is weakest. That does not weaken the trial; it strengthens the case for asking what inside the package did the work. A guardrail that improves post-test scores has still produced a package, and the strongest available evidence for guardrails is also the cleanest available instance of the problem this paper addresses.

The distinction we draw is therefore this: a study that reduces leakage and improves learning simultaneously has produced a *package*, and a package does not identify which of its components did the work. Separating the contribution of scaffolding from the contribution of merely leaking less requires the two to be switchable independently — which is a design requirement, not an analysis choice.

Two lines of work come close enough that the difference must be drawn rather than assumed. Kadir (2026) performs component attribution, but attributes *failures to pipeline components* — selection, generation, verification, enforcement — not shares of an outcome to instructional channels. Fan et al. (2026) hold the base model and the simulated student fixed and contrast two policies, which isolates the policy as a cause of a difference in judged pedagogy without decomposing an outcome gain into a scaffolding part and a leak-suppression part. Both are attributive in a sense; neither is attributive in the sense §2.4.3 needs. No system reviewed in §2.3 is built so that the two channels can be switched independently and the residual reported as a share, and none supplies a test by which the attribution can fail.

### 2.4.4 Cryptography education and GenAI tutoring: a crowded adjacent scene

The application scenario adopted in this paper is **cryptography education at the graduate level**, so the nearby literature on GenAI tutoring for cybersecurity and cryptography deserves explicit comparison rather than silent avoidance. It is also, candidly, **more crowded than the 408 setting we left**: the gap we occupy is in *method* (attribution), not in *scene*.

Mukherjee, Le and Chow (2025, *Future Internet* 17(4): 154, DOI 10.3390/fi17040154), from the Institute of Cybersecurity and Cryptology at the University of Wollongong, present a **multi-agent LLM tutoring system for a graduate cybersecurity programme** and demonstrate it in that programme's courses — a direct overlap in level (graduate), domain (cybersecurity/cryptography), and mechanism (multi-agent orchestration of specialised LLMs). Their evaluation is **survey-based**: content quality, platform usability, and perceived effectiveness; they state that empirical validation remains a task for future research (verified against the open-access text, 2026-09-21). We report this precisely rather than implying a learning-outcome evaluation they did not claim, because the difference we draw is one of question, not of evidential strength. García, Recio-Colmenares, Recio-Colmenares, Mejia-Lucatero and Fernández-Robles (2026), of the Universidad de Guadalajara, devote a chapter to *Integrating Generative AI for Personalized Learning in Cryptography Education*: eight academic terms (2022A–2025A) of the undergraduate cryptography course IH063, in which the LLM is positioned as **adaptive scaffolding**. The chapter states an exploratory design without a control cohort and reports an initial performance dip followed by stabilisation, not a causal effect. Al Foori and Oyelere (2026, *Frontiers in Computer Science* 8:1826732) contribute **AI-CES**, a lecturer-in-the-loop platform for scenario-based cybersecurity education. It is an original-research article, not a review, and its evaluation is a single-site exploratory pilot (10 lecturers, 20 students, post-use self-report surveys) that the authors position "as an exploratory pilot rather than as a controlled effectiveness trial" and of which they state that it "does not establish causal effects on learning outcomes" (full text verified 2026-09-22). Domestically, 《基于生成式人工智能的密码学教学新模式》(《计算机教育》) uses elliptic-curve teaching as a worked example and validates with a questionnaire.

**Why this does not displace our claim.** Every one of these systems is built as a **teaching system or mode**; none performs the attribution decomposition that §2.4.3 identifies as the field's open problem. The IGI chapter even casts the LLM as scaffolding **without controlling leakage**. Mukherjee et al. train and orchestrate agents but report no separation of scaffolding effect from leak-suppression effect, no continuous leakage–intensity curve, and no mutation test of the attribution. Switching scene to cryptography therefore **weakens scene novelty while leaving our first claim untouched** — and it raises the bar on honestly stating the scene's prior art, which we do here. The reviewer who asks "how is this different from Mukherjee et al. (2025)?" is answered by §2.4.3, not by the choice of domain.

## 2.5 Why learner diagnosis and knowledge tracing are not claimed as contributions here

A reviewer reading a system with a knowledge graph and a learner model will ask why diagnosis is not claimed as a contribution. The answer is that the subfield is mature, is being surveyed, and has documented validity problems that this paper does not solve — and that what it lacks is precisely the attribution layer described above.

The lineage is long, which is part of the point: model-tracing tutors have represented a learner's knowledge state and used it to select the next step since the Cognitive Tutors work of the mid-1990s (Anderson, Corbett, Koedinger, & Pelletier, 1995), so a knowledge graph plus a learner model is an established configuration rather than a contribution. Cognitive diagnosis and knowledge tracing now also support a dedicated 2026 survey (Chen et al., *Neurocomputing* 697:134160), which is the ordinary signal that a subfield has closed. Architectures of the kind a reader might suppose to be novel here have already been named and published: Yasir et al. (2026, BEA 2026), benchmarking seven LLM feedback agents against knowledge-graph-derived ground truth over 10,836 solution–feedback pairs in propositional logic, separate diagnosis from scaffolding **by architecture** — their conclusion being that "LLMs are better suited for hybrid architectures where KG-grounded models handle diagnosis while LLMs support open-ended scaffolding and dialogue." Dedicated benchmarks exist (Lee, Lee, Jeong, Lee, Shin and Kwon, 2026, EduClaw-Bench — an agent tutor paired with a **knowledge-tracing-grounded simulated learner** over a 30-day horizon), as does work on the privacy dimension (Boyapati, Yu, Jiang and Zhan, 2026). More importantly, three empirical results argue against treating diagnosis as a solved input to instruction. Schuetze, Yan and Carvalho (2025) find that BKT, BKT-with-forgetting and AFM fit retrospectively at AUC ≈ 0.74–0.79 but, under temporal cross-validation — training on earlier data to predict later performance, the deployment condition — **consistently overestimate future performance by 47–58%**, fail to capture spacing effects, and in some cases rank practice conditions incorrectly. An, McLaren and Stamper (2026) show that the two-state assumption behind BKT is violated by deceptive over-generalisation: a learner can appear to have mastered a skill and then systematically misapply it when a hidden application constraint is violated, so *correct is not the same as competent*. Yasir et al. (2026, BEA 2026) additionally report that accurate diagnosis did not reliably produce pedagogically actionable feedback, and that LLM tutors confirm correct steps reliably while over-rejecting valid reasoning and over-affirming erroneous reasoning.

Chinese-language work on diagnostic systems makes the same demand at the level of design. Xiong (2026) argues that teacher-training practice lacks a complete diagnosis–training–feedback–optimisation loop and that its evaluation "degenerates into formality" (评价多流于形式), and her system's remedy is a quantified diagnostic report that makes strengths and gaps explicit and tracks improvement over time. The subject differs and must be stated: that system diagnoses teachers' instructional design and lecture performance, whereas SAGE diagnoses a student's knowledge points. The mechanism is shared; the subject is not, and the analogy should not be pressed further.

**Position taken in this paper.** Diagnosis and knowledge tracing are used as supporting components — an existing cryptography knowledge graph anchors bottleneck localisation and exercise selection — and are not claimed as contributions. Nor is the coupling of a learner state to instruction claimed as novel here: ScaffoldLM (Li, Zhu, Wang, Li and Huang, 2026, ACL) infers the learner's cognitive state in a control loop and selects tutoring actions from it, in a published system, and EduDial supplies a corpus and an eleven-dimensional instrument for teaching ability at a scale no contribution of ours matches (§2.3.1). The knowledge graph in SAGE is a localisation device, not a claim. What we claim instead is the layer this mature subfield does not supply: a design in which the effect of scaffolding can be separated from the effect of withholding. That is a claim about attribution, and it is orthogonal to how well the learner is modelled.

## 2.6 Summary of position

The field has established that answer-oriented assistance harms learning, has produced tutors that withhold answers at every placement catalogued in §2.3 — four architectural layers and, by 2026, a measurement and audit layer that scores whether the withholding holds — and has demonstrated — across three independent research groups and one product-scale deployment — that constraints placed at or around generation can be routed around by learners. It has not exposed guidance strength as a tunable quantity, has not measured leakage against such a quantity, and has not separated scaffolding effects from leak-suppression effects in the gains it reports. The last of these is the one the field identified for itself. SAGE is designed so that the separation is possible; §3 states the framework, §4–§5 the design and measurement, and §6 reports the attributed share.

One further comparison is owed, because a reader who knows the 2026 literature will ask for it. The nearest published neighbour we have located is **EduClaw-Bench** (Lee, Lee, Jeong, Lee, Shin & Kwon, 2026), which pairs an agent tutor with a **knowledge-tracing-grounded simulated learner** over a **30-day horizon** and reports, as its preprint describes it, both a *negligible* answer-leakage rate and helpfulness ratings that agree between live and simulated classrooms. Two elements of that configuration we did not expect to share — a KT-grounded simulated learner and a long horizon — and we say so plainly rather than claiming the configuration as our own. Our difference from it is narrow and we state it as such: EduClaw, as described, checks leakage as a property that holds, whereas SAGE **tunes** the constraint and then **decomposes** the resulting gain into its scaffolding and leak-suppression shares. Checking that a constraint holds and measuring what a constrained system teaches are different operations, and §2.4.3 argues that only the second licenses an attribution claim.

**★ Two red lines added 2026-09-21, after this study became simulation-only.** These now bite hardest and must be checked at every revision pass:

1. **Never imply that we ran a classroom study.** The paper makes **no classroom claim**. Any sentence that reads as though a classroom study exists is a **fabrication, not a stylistic slip**. → Consequence: `15` §6.2 must be re-written as a *simulation-design* section; leaving it as a `NO DATA` placeholder for a study that will never run is not acceptable, because the placeholder itself asserts that the study is pending.

2. **Never treat the presence of real student data as a measure of another study's quality.** Our own contribution is methodological and validated in simulation. The critique must stay where it is **defensible**: prior systems do not *separate* scaffolding from leak suppression. That is a **design omission** irrespective of whether the data came from students or from a simulator, and it is therefore a fair criticism to make from a simulation-only position.
