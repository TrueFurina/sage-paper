## 7.1 What the attribution result does and does not license

**The curve is not the finding.** The result a reader is likely to carry away from §6 is the shape of the relationship: as scaffolding intensity rises, answer leakage falls monotonically while earned independent solves rise, peak, and fall again. It is tempting to read that shape as evidence that guidance works. It is not, and the reason is the whole point of this paper.

A tutor that leaks less *and* whose learners do better has varied two things at once. That is the package problem of §2.4.3, and it is not solved by having measured both quantities — it is solved only by a condition in which one moves without the other.

The clearest instance of the package, and the one that makes the point impossible to wave away, is also the strongest study in this literature. Bastani et al. (2025, *PNAS*) randomised classrooms — nearly a thousand secondary-school students in about fifty classes — across an unguarded chatbot, a guardrailed version of the same model whose prompt instructed it to give hints without directly giving the answer, and a no-tool control, and found that the guarded condition kept the practice gain and removed the later-test loss. On any ordinary reading that is a decisive result in favour of guardrails, and we do not qualify it. It is also a package, and a three-channel one at that: the guardrail changed what the tutor said, stopped it handing over the answer, and — because the prompt also carried teacher-supplied correct solutions and prescribed feedback on common mistakes — removed a source of incorrect feedback that the unguarded tutor retained. The trial's design — treatment against control — has no condition in which any one of those moved alone. A reader who infers from it that guidance was the active ingredient has performed the substitution this paper is built to prevent, and has done so on the basis of a large randomised trial, which is precisely why the substitution is dangerous rather than merely careless. Our claim is not that Bastani et al. should have separated the two; it is that the separation is a further question, that it needs a further arm, and that until it is asked the result supports "guardrails help" and not "guidance helps." Arm B is that condition. Under our stipulated learner model it moves the outcome by **+0.0594** against the **+0.4380** produced by the full intervention, which is what the **86%** attributable share means: about seven-eighths of the interior improvement comes from guidance doing work, and about one-seventh would have been obtained by withholding alone. The number is large, and it is stable across the sensitivity range (76–88%). Neither property is the claim. **The claim is that the decomposition is well-defined and that the diagnostic rejects when it should.** The latter is what mutation testing establishes: removing scaffolding's mastery gain collapses the effect, and the diagnostic refuses the proposition.

**The share is not a constant of nature; it is a function of how instructive a leaked answer is.** This is the most important qualification, and it is not a caveat bolted on afterwards — it is built into the apparatus as a mutant. When leaked answers are made genuinely instructive, the scaffolding-attributable share falls below the 50% bar and the diagnostic rejects the proposition; the response is smooth and monotone, crossing the bar at a leak-durability between **0.20 and 0.22** and continuing down to **25%** by 0.70 (§6.3.1). Our headline number therefore holds *under the stipulation that a revealed answer does not teach*. Whether that stipulation is true of real learners is an open empirical question, and it is not a settled one in the learner's favour or against: a complete worked solution is also the canonical form of a worked example, and under some conditions learners demonstrably learn from worked examples. If real learners are nearer the instructive-leak end of that range than our model assumes, the attributable share is smaller than 86% and might be much smaller. We do not know where real learners sit, and we do not present 86% as an estimate of that quantity.

**Separability is an architectural precondition, not an analysis choice.** The decomposition exists only because scaffolding and withholding are carried by different agents and can therefore be switched independently (§4.1). In a design where a single component both declines to answer and guides — which is what a prompt-level constraint does — arm B cannot be constructed at all, and the package is the most that can be reported, however carefully it is measured. This is why §4.1 is written as part of the attribution design rather than as implementation detail, and it is the sense in which our contribution is prior to measurement: we are describing a property a tutoring architecture must have for its evaluation to be interpretable. The 13 mutation checks and the resolution floor of §5.2.2 are the same point from the other side — the apparatus fails loudly when a mechanism it depends on is removed, and it fails *quietly but wrongly* when run below the resolution at which it can see.

What §6 therefore licenses is this: under a learner model in which we stipulate that leaked answers are not instructive, the separation is large and stable, and the diagnostic refuses every model in which the separation does not hold. What it does not license is any statement that scaffolding beat withholding among students.

## 7.2 The boundary is where assistance stops being assistance

**Two opposite failures bound the useful range.** Scaffolding is support calibrated to what a learner cannot yet do and withdrawn as competence grows; a scaffold that is never calibrated and never withdrawn is not a scaffold. Under that reading the two sides of the §6 curve are not two degrees of the same thing. To the left, the tutor performs the task: at λ = 0 there are **0.670** leak-solves against **0.128** earned solves, so most apparent success is bought. To the right, the tutor's own output collapses: at λ = 0.8, **0.735** of problems are never solved by any route, and the curve does not continue to decay — it flattens (0.2642 at λ = 0.70 against 0.2651 at λ = 0.80, overlapping intervals). The learner is not being challenged there; they are being left alone with the problem. The relationship is non-monotonic because two different failure modes bound the range, not because there is an interior point to be found.

**A pedagogical reading of the collapse.** The constraint-collapse literature we adopt the functional form from finds a discrete strategy switch with a floor — a planning failure rather than a capability limit, recoverable by two-pass generation (§3.2). Pedagogically, that is the signature of a tutor that has stopped teaching while continuing to withhold: it still refuses the answer but no longer supplies the element the learner cannot supply. Difficulty is productive only while the learner can still act on it (Kapur, 2008); past the boundary they cannot, so the struggle stops being productive and becomes impossibility. This matters because the two readings have different operational consequences. If over-restraint were a gradual loss of helpfulness, tuning would be the right response. Because it is a switch, there is nothing to tune beyond it.

**The operational recommendation is to monitor, not to tune — and we decline to recommend a setting.** The boundary's location moved over **λ\* ∈ [0.20, 0.45]** as we varied constants we stipulated and never measured, so λ\* = 0.30 is a description of one configuration, not an operating point. What appears to be stable is not the knob but the *delivered* quantity: when the grid is solved so that the delivered fraction of the scaffold's content is held constant, the optimum sits at the same delivered fraction (≈0.94) regardless of collapse depth, and most of the remaining movement disappears once the grid is expressed in distance-to-boundary units. An implementation can observe how much of its intended guidance actually reaches the learner; it cannot observe a constant it has not measured. The practical instruction is therefore to instrument delivery and watch the distance to the cliff, which is the concrete form of the "differently calibrated" guidance the field has asked for (Brender et al., 2026, §5.1) and the reason we have avoided the language of an optimal intensity throughout.

**The question in our title is decided at the first hint, not the last.** *When* guidance still helps is a question about timing as well as intensity. Hint requests behave as a cascade rather than as isolated events: in one geometry ITS dataset, the most likely next action after a first hint is to request a second (87% of the time), and after the second, a third (88%); counting a further request as failure, success falls from 78% on the first attempt to 21% after the first hint (Goldin, Koedinger & Aleven, 2012, Table 2). A system that guards only the final rung has already conceded the exchange. This is why the interception of §4.3 sits at the first request and degrades into a sub-problem rather than refusing.

**Why this domain, stated plainly.** Cryptography was chosen for an instrumentation reason: proximity to a complete answer is graded *natively* in the discipline — a lemma is further from a proof than a derivation step, which is further than the key step — and each grade can be anchored to a node of a knowledge graph, so "how close did the tutor come" is an annotatable property of a turn rather than a judgement call (§4.2). That is an advantage for measurement, and it is the only sense in which the domain is load-bearing. We report nothing about cryptography students, and we do not claim the apparatus requires this domain: we claim that instrumenting it is easier here than in a domain where the grading would have to be constructed by hand.

## 7.3 How this relates to work already published

Our first claim is about measurement, and four studies sit close enough to it that the difference has to be drawn rather than assumed. None of them is displaced by this paper, and we say so in each case. The first of them is the closest thing to a prior version of this paper that we have found, and we put it first rather than last.

### 7.3.1 Pisan (2026): the nearest prior work, and what we concede to it

Pisan (2026, arXiv:2608.12292) reports a supervisor architecture that teaches an LLM tutor to withhold the answer, with an evidence-driven method for tuning Socratic behaviour: a per-turn contract, a deterministic checker and an LLM judge, an eight-level help ladder, and a deployed system. It is the closest work to ours that we have located, and the overlap should not be minimised — it addresses the same proposition, it is deployed where ours is simulated, and its withholding guarantees are machine-checked at three layers where ours are simulated constraints on a stipulated model.

We concede the substance of it. On the question *can a tutor be made to hold the answer, and can the holding be made measurable and tunable*, Pisan has more to show than we do, and we do not claim otherwise. Two further concessions follow. Their ladder is finer-grained than ours (eight levels against three). And like us they report no human subjects, which removes any argument we might otherwise have made about a competitor's lack of classroom evidence — the criticism we level at the field in §2.4.3 cannot be pointed at this paper and then quietly exempted from ourselves.

What remains is one question, and we restrict our claim to it. Pisan establishes that withholding can be enforced and its strength tuned. It does not ask, of a system that withholds and whose learners improve, what part of that improvement came from the guidance and what part came from the withholding. A supervisor architecture that enforces a contract is not a design in which the withholding channel can be moved while the guidance channel is held fixed, so the decomposition we report cannot be read off it — not because the work is weaker, but because the question is different. The two are not in competition on the axis that matters most to us; enforcement and attribution are answers to different questions about the same mechanism.

That difference is also the reason we regard this work as an opportunity rather than only a threat. The apparatus of §6 is indifferent to how withholding was implemented, provided the two channels can be reconstructed from logs. A system of this kind — deployed, instrumented, with a machine-checked per-turn record of what was withheld — is closer to being able to supply the withholding-only arm than anything we could build ourselves, and running our decomposition over logs from a deployment like this one is the most direct route to the validation that §7.4 says we have not performed.

### 7.3.2 SocraticPO: a reward-side counterpart, not a competitor

SocraticPO (Liu et al., 2026) decays the reward for assisted success so that the **student policy** being trained by reinforcement learning does not learn to wait for help, with the decay self-determined from historical statistics rather than set by a tunable parameter. Its student is a policy, not a learner, and its evaluation is on benchmark tasks; we cite it for the mechanism and for its own diagnosis of the prompt layer, not as an educational evaluation. It works on the **reward** side; ours works on the **content** side — what the tutor is permitted to say. The two are not in competition, and the relation is closer to a dual than to a rivalry: SocraticPO changes whether help is *credited*, SAGE bounds what help *contains*. Their §5.4 supplies the sharpest statement of why the content side cannot be left to prompting — a teacher with ground-truth access "may still leak solution-specific hints" even when the prompt forbids it, and this is "inherently limited by the teacher model's instruction-following ability" — and the same paper observes empirically that guidance "can become negative when the teacher turns the answer into overly direct hints." That is our phenomenon, independently observed, at the point where our ladder's upper rungs sit. What it does not supply is a relation to an adjustable constraint: it reports hint behaviour for one trained configuration rather than varying a constraint and plotting the response. That is not a claim about the whole training strand, and we do not make it one: **TutorRL** (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025, EMNLP) does vary a leakage penalty λ and does plot leakage against it. What TutorRL does not do is decompose a durable gain, which is the property the apparatus exists to supply. We do not count the absence of a tutoring instrument in a policy-optimisation paper as a gap in that work — that inference appeared in an earlier draft of this paper and is withdrawn (§2.4.2). Our λ–leakage relation supplies the functional form, and it is a simulation result. A system combining both mechanisms is a natural next step, and the apparatus would then say which side produced the gain — which is the useful property of having it.

### 7.3.3 Mukherjee et al. (2025): the same scene, a different question

Mukherjee, Le and Chow (2025) present a multi-agent LLM tutoring system for a graduate cybersecurity programme and demonstrate it in that programme's courses. The overlap with our application scenario is direct — graduate level, cryptography/cybersecurity, multi-agent orchestration — and we do not dress it up: **choosing this scene weakens our scene novelty, and we say so rather than waiting for a reviewer to say it.** What does not overlap is the claim. Theirs is a system; ours is a measurement apparatus. Their evaluation is a survey-based assessment of content quality, platform usability and perceived effectiveness, and they state empirical validation of learning outcomes as a task for future research; ours is a simulation of an attribution apparatus. The contrast is therefore **not** one of evidential strength — a deployment and a perception survey are things this paper does not have — but one of question: a tutor can be well evaluated on perceived effectiveness without ever separating the contribution of scaffolding from the contribution of withholding, and that separation is what our apparatus exists to produce. They do not report it, and a system not built around switchable channels cannot. The adjacent work makes the contrast sharper rather than fuzzier: the García et al. (2026) chapter on GenAI for cryptography education explicitly casts the LLM as *adaptive scaffolding* while controlling nothing about leakage — an unusually clean instance of the package of §2.4.3, in which the field's own vocabulary names one mechanism while the design varies two. The reviewer who asks how this differs from Mukherjee et al. is answered by §2.4.3 and §6.1.3, not by the choice of domain. We would add that their deployment is something this paper does not have, and that our apparatus could in principle be run over logs from a system like theirs — which is a complement, not a substitute.

### 7.3.4 Suvernev et al. (2026): a trainable constraint is not new, and we do not claim it

Suvernev et al. (2026) train hint models (1B–7B) with supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with GRPO, and an adversarial curriculum, and deploy them in a first-year programming course of 600 students. A trainable withholding constraint is therefore **not** ours to claim, and our adversarial robustness term has a clear antecedent in their adversarial curriculum. We hold only the published abstract, so we state this as described there: their safety mechanism is a jailbreak-prevention filter and their optimisation target is hint quality; no leakage measure appears in the abstract, and we have not been able to verify one. The difference we do claim against Suvernev specifically is one of kind rather than priority — a binary gate versus a graded, continuously adjustable constraint whose relationship to an observed leakage rate is reported — and it is a difference in what is *measured*, not in whether a classroom was used. The graded form itself is not ours either: TutorRL (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025) already reports leakage and solve rate as curves against a continuously varied λ. What we add is the other half — an outcome measured after the tutor is withdrawn, and a decomposition of that gain into the scaffolding and withholding channels. They have a real course and real students; we have a simulation. Claiming a better evaluation would be indefensible, and we do not.

## 7.4 What a designer should take away

These are implications of the design, not findings about learners, and each is defensible from a simulation-only position.

1. **Expose guidance strength as a parameter and log it.** No system reviewed in §2.3 exposes it continuously; without it, "how much guidance" is not a reproducible treatment condition, and reproducibility is the condition that 26% of the comparisons audited by Weidlich et al. (2025) failed.
2. **Ship the withholding-only arm.** Any evaluation of a guidance tutor that omits it reports a package. The arm costs one condition and converts an uninterpretable gain into a decomposition.
3. **Instrument leakage with an empirical measure, and do not use the training penalty as one.** A quantity that pins to its ceiling whether leakage is certain or absent is measuring its own functional form (§8.2).
4. **Place the constraint at the first hint, and degrade rather than refuse** (§4.3): the cascade is one-way, and misreading a struggling learner as an answer-seeker is the more damaging error.
5. **Monitor delivery and distance to the boundary; do not tune toward a peak** (§7.2). The peak's location is conditional on constants nobody has measured.
6. **Report the attributable share with its sensitivity, not the improvement alone.** A gain that could have been produced by withholding less is a different result from one that could not.

The six points above are addressed to someone building a system. A reader of this journal is more likely to be asking a different question: what does a simulation of a measurement apparatus offer someone who studies learning, given that it offers no finding about learners? The answer we would defend is narrow but not empty.

**What is offered is a protocol, not an effect.** The literature this paper sits in evaluates guidance tutors by comparing a guided condition against a less guided one and reporting an outcome difference. Where the guided condition also withholds more — which is the normal case, because withholding is how guidance is implemented — that difference is a package, and the convention is to credit it to guidance. Nothing in the conventional design can contradict that credit. The apparatus here changes what a report of this kind has to contain: with a switchable withholding channel, the same outcome difference decomposes into a scaffolding term and a leak-suppression term, and the second term is not assumed to be zero but measured. Its value therefore does not depend on the 86% being right. It depends on the decomposition being well-defined and on the diagnostic refusing when the decomposition fails, both of which are properties of the design rather than of the number.

**What would make it useful is someone running it on data we do not control.** We have shown the apparatus is internally coherent and that it rejects the models it should reject. We have not shown that it recovers a true attribution on empirical data, because no data set exists in which the scaffolding contribution to an LLM tutor's effect is known by independent means, and we did not manufacture one. The apparatus is in that sense unfinished, and we would rather say so than let a reader infer a validation that was never performed. The cheapest credible next step is to run it over logs from an existing deployment — a system of the kind Pisan (2026) reports, with its machine-checked per-turn record of what was withheld, would be close to ideal — where the two channels can be reconstructed from transcripts and the share can be estimated with double-coded leakage. That is an empirical study, and this paper is not it.

Stated in one sentence: this paper contributes a way of making a class of evaluation results interpretable, demonstrates that the way is coherent and reject-capable in simulation, and leaves its accuracy on real data as an open question it has made answerable rather than one it has answered.

One further point is worth making for this journal's context. Where national policy frames the objective as capability development rather than knowledge transmission (能力提升为本; Hu Xiaohong, 2026), the outcome of interest is what the learner can do without the tool, not whether the task was completed with it. That makes the earned-independent-solve outcome the right primary measure — but it is a reason for choosing the measure, not evidence that any system achieves it, and we offer no such evidence here.

## 7.5 The argument in one paragraph, and where it would break

Guidance helps between two boundaries: below the lower one the tutor does the work, above the upper one it stops supplying what the learner cannot supply. Whether a tutor's benefit is scaffolding rather than mere withholding is not visible in its outcomes and requires a second arm in which withholding moves alone; under a stipulated learner model that arm leaves about one-seventh of the improvement unexplained by guidance, and the diagnostic that produces this number refuses every model in which the separation fails. The argument would break in four places, all of them stated as limitations rather than repaired here: if the two channels are entangled in a real implementation, so that no withholding-only arm can be built (§8.6); if leaked answers are instructive for real learners, in which case the share falls toward the 25–30% that instructive-leak settings produce and we do not know where real learners sit (§7.1, §6.3.1); if a simulated learner is more compliant than a real one, which inflates any scaffolding effect (§8.3); and if the constants governing the boundary are measured and move it, which affects the location but not the existence of the boundary or the share (§8.1). The first three are empirical questions about learners and implementations. This paper does not answer them; it supplies the design that makes them answerable.
