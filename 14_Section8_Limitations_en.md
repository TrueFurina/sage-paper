## 8.1 The withholding boundary is located under stipulated constants

The boundary reported in §6 is conditional on a functional form and on three constants that no study has measured. Following the constraint-collapse literature (Potraghloo et al., 2026, arXiv 2604.13006, §4.1), we model the cost of withholding as a threshold rather than a gradient: that work reports, across seven instruction-tuned models spanning five families, that the cost of an output constraint takes the form of a discrete strategy switch with a collapse floor rather than continuous degradation, and that the mechanism is a planning failure that two-pass generation largely repairs. **The adoption is an analogy, not evidence from our setting.** That source contains no measure of answer leakage, no tutoring scenario, no notion of scaffolding intensity and no learner of any kind; a full-text search finds none of those terms. It therefore establishes the *shape* of the cost and not its *magnitude* — and this is one reason the three constants below are stipulated rather than measured. The three constants governing that shape in our model — collapse depth, boundary location, and boundary sharpness — are **stipulated values, not measured quantities**, and so are the learner-model constants governing knowledge durability and frustration.

Two consequences follow, and they are of very different severity. The **location** of the boundary is sensitive to these constants: across the range we examined, the located boundary moves by more than a factor of two, and we therefore report its position as an interval rather than as a point. The **existence** of the boundary and the **attributable share** are not sensitive to *these* constants: mutation testing rejects the models in which withholding makes no difference or in which leaked answers teach, and it preserves support across the stipulated range. **The attributable share is not, however, insensitive to every constant we stipulated.** One parameter governs how much durable mastery a leaked answer confers, and it is not among the three discussed above because its effect is of a different kind: it does not shift the boundary, it moves the share itself across the bar that decides the claim. It is reported separately in §8.1.1. Readers should therefore take from §6 that a boundary exists and that most of the interior improvement is attributable to scaffolding, and should *not* take a specific setting as a recommended operating point. This is why we have restated the quantity as a withholding boundary and have avoided the language of an optimal intensity throughout: a deployed system should monitor its distance to a cliff, not its proximity to a peak it cannot reliably locate.

### 8.1.1 The attributable share rests on one unmeasured assumption

The parameter is `leak_durable`: how much durable mastery a learner retains from an answer that was simply given to them. At baseline it is set to 0.012, which encodes the position that a revealed answer teaches almost nothing. It does not affect where the boundary is located — the located boundary is λ\* = 0.30 at every value we scanned — but it determines how much of the interior improvement can be credited to guidance, and it is the only constant we have found that can carry that credit below the level at which the attribution claim fails. Scanned at the configuration of the main result, the share falls monotonically from 86% to 25% and crosses the decisive bar between 0.20 and 0.22 (§6.3.1).

We have no empirical warrant for the value 0.012 and we do not claim one. One external result does bear on its **direction**, and we report it as directional support rather than as a measurement. Bastani et al. (2025, *PNAS*) randomised classrooms — nearly a thousand secondary-school mathematics students in about fifty classes — across an unguarded chatbot and a guardrailed version of the same model whose prompt instructed it to give hints without directly giving the answer; students using the unguarded chatbot scored higher while practising and **17% lower on a later test taken without the tool**. Gains bought from disclosed answers therefore did not survive the removal of the tool, which is exactly what a small value of this parameter asserts. We stop short of calling this a calibration: the trial does not estimate the parameter, it is set in school mathematics with a chatbot rather than in graduate cryptography with a tutor, and its guarded condition changed guidance, withholding and feedback accuracy together — its prompt also carried teacher-supplied correct solutions and prescribed feedback — and so cannot separate those channels either. The direction is supported; the magnitude remains stipulated. The honest statement of our first claim is therefore conditional, and we state it as such wherever the number appears: under the stipulation that a revealed answer teaches almost nothing, the attributable share is 86%; to the extent that real learners retain more from a revealed answer than that, the share is smaller, and the scan shows by how much.

This is the assumption on which the paper's first claim most directly rests, and we name it rather than distributing it among the other stipulated constants, where its different character — it moves the claim, not the location — would be lost. Estimating it, rather than stipulating it, is part of the future work of §8.7 and is a prerequisite for any use of this apparatus on real data.

## 8.2 Leakage is measured by human coding, not by the training objective

The penalty that shapes the tutor's policy during training is not a measurement instrument, and this paper does not present it as one. In our simulation the implemented estimator is degenerate as a measurement of leaked information: because the modelled emission probability is a deterministic monotone function of the learner's effective mastery, its squared correlation is approximately one by construction (measured at 0.99988–0.99999 across the whole intensity range), and the estimate pins to its clipping ceiling at every setting — including the setting at which the empirical leak rate has fallen to 0.0003. An estimator that returns the same value when leakage is near-certain and when it is absent is measuring its own functional form.

Leakage is therefore reported using empirical measures only: answer-leakage rate and *Telling@N*. Both require human judgement about whether an utterance reveals the answer. Within the present simulation that judgement is not made by coders: the reported leakage values are computed from the modelled emission probability and are therefore properties of the stipulated model rather than independent estimates (§3.2, §6.2). In any future classroom extension they would be double-coded with agreement statistics reported (§8.7), and the attributable share would inherit the reliability of that coding. The reliability burden is thus real but deferred: it is not borne by the numbers in §6, and it is the reason those numbers are labelled as simulated throughout. We regard this as the correct trade — a bounded but noisy measure is more informative than a clean one that is provably not measuring anything — but it is a limitation, and the coding reliability attained in §6 should be read alongside the effect sizes rather than assumed away.

**One risk to our own formulation is untested here, and we name it rather than leaving it implicit.** Wang et al. (2026), aligning a 7B model to Socratic tutoring with GRPO, report that their best-performing variant was the one that *omitted* the directness penalty, and suggest that explicit anti-leakage terms "can conflict with gradient-based behavioral alignment." Our objective places exactly such a term in the loss (§3.2). The present study is a simulation without a gradient-based training loop: it specifies the constraint's effect directly rather than learning it, and so it cannot test whether that term helps or hurts under real optimisation. We therefore do not claim the term is beneficial in general, and we record this as an open risk that a trained instantiation of SAGE would have to resolve, not as a limitation we have characterised.

## 8.3 Simulated learners cannot support claims about learning

The simulation evidence in this paper concerns the behaviour of a model under stipulated assumptions about learners. It is evidence about the *apparatus* — that the diagnostic can distinguish scaffolding effects from leak-suppression effects under known conditions — and not evidence that any learner learned anything.

This caution is not routine. Recent work on simulated students reports that a simulated learner will abandon an assigned misconception after corrective feedback **whether or not that feedback addressed the misconception**, which makes ineffective instruction appear effective under simulation. Any simulation in which the learner's response to feedback is more compliant than a real learner's will overstate the effect of scaffolding. Our simulation therefore cannot be used to argue that learners in general benefit from guidance, and we do not use it that way: every simulation result is labelled as such. **This paper reports no classroom data at all (D20 path ②), so it makes no claims about learning; a classroom study, should one be run, would be the only source of such claims, and it is explicitly flagged as future work (§8.7) rather than pooled with the simulation.**

## 8.4 One discipline, one course, one term

The present evidence is a fully simulated study set in a **single subject domain — the graduate cryptography curriculum** — anchored to the cryptography knowledge graph (§19). The knowledge graph that anchors the learner model is specific to that domain, and the prerequisite structure that makes bottleneck localisation possible had to be built for it. Whether the attributable share observed here generalises to domains with weaker prerequisite structure, or to learners at different levels of prior attainment, is not addressed. The simulation is also stylised: the learner model is stipulated and does not capture the full heterogeneity of real learners, and given the field's insistence on measuring learning after assistance is withdrawn, a delayed-post-test classroom validation is the natural next step rather than an optional refinement.

## 8.5 Sources we could not obtain, and how our wording is restricted by that

Two sources material to our framing were not available to us in full, and our claims are worded to the strength of what we actually hold. Suvernev et al. (2026) is behind a publisher paywall; we hold its published abstract, and every statement we make about its mechanism carries the qualification *as described in the published abstract* — in particular, we do not claim that its system performs no leakage measurement, only that we have not been able to verify one. We have since traced the "cognitive debt" study to its source (Kosmyna et al., 2025, arXiv:2506.08872, MIT Media Lab), and doing so obliged us to weaken one of our own sentences: the abstract supports reduced neural engagement, low self-reported ownership and poor recall of recently written text, but not the "reduced critical evaluation" our earlier draft attributed to it. The traced study nonetheless remains an unreviewed preprint whose fourth-session crossover rests on 18 of 54 participants; we therefore rest the causal claim on Liu et al. (2026), whose randomised design (N = 1,222) measures performance after assistance is withdrawn, and use the EEG study only as convergent neural evidence.

A third source was withdrawn rather than qualified. **Udeshi et al. (2026)** — a report on the Khanmigo deployment that we had used in §1.3 as product-scale evidence that learners route around answer-withholding — reached us as conference session notes and could not be located in any of the four bibliographic databases we searched. We have therefore removed it as a source of evidence rather than retaining it under a hedge, and the classroom-scale circumvention finding is now carried by Zhao, Knežević and Käser (2026), whose adversarial benchmark is peer-reviewed and independently locatable. Where a claim in §1–§2 depends on a source we hold only partially, the qualification travels with the claim rather than sitting once in this section.

## 8.6 What would change our conclusions

It is worth stating plainly which findings are fragile. The attribution apparatus does not depend on the correctness of our learner model: it depends only on the two channels being independently switchable, and a better learner model would sharpen the estimate without altering the design. The boundary's *location* does depend on constants we stipulated, and a measured set of those constants would move it — possibly substantially. The attributable share would fall if a substantial part of the improvement were produced by mechanisms our decomposition does not separate, such as effects of practice volume; the classroom design holds time and content across conditions to limit this, but the possibility is not eliminated by the simulation. Finally, the metacognitive component is measured as a set of log-derived behavioural indicators rather than as a validated construct, and we do not present it as one; a claim that SAGE improves metacognition would require a measurement design this study does not have.

## 8.7 Future work

Four directions follow directly from the above. Measuring the stipulated constants empirically, which would convert the boundary's location from a conditional estimate into a located one. **A classroom validation of the apparatus in a real cryptography course**, extended across a second domain and a delayed post-test, which addresses §8.4 and the field's requirement to measure capability after assistance ends. Establishing coding reliability for leakage at scale, and where feasible moving toward measurement that does not depend on human judgement of individual utterances. And coupling the attribution apparatus to learner models developed elsewhere, since the apparatus is indifferent to how the learner is represented and would be more useful if it were not tied to one knowledge graph.
